Safety Insights

Explore the factors influencing fire safety in spacecraft and the scientific research helping protect astronauts during future missions.

🌌

Microgravity

In microgravity, buoyancy-driven convection is strongly reduced. Hot gases do not rise in the same way as on Earth. Flame shape and the movement of combustion products can change, while ventilation becomes especially important.

🫧

Oxygen Concentration

Oxygen supports combustion. Its concentration, together with pressure, fuel properties and environmental conditions, can influence ignition and flame spread. A higher oxygen concentration may increase fire hazards for some materials.

💨

Ventilation and Airflow

Spacecraft use mechanical ventilation to move air. Airflow can transport oxygen, heat and smoke, and can influence flame behavior. Its effects depend on the material and experimental conditions.

🧯

Fire Protection

Spacecraft fire protection combines prevention, material selection, detection, emergency procedures and suppression systems. Fire safety must be evaluated using validated engineering standards and mission-specific requirements.

🧪

NASA Combustion Research

NASA experiments such as FLEX investigate liquid fuel droplet combustion and flame extinction. BASS investigations examine burning and flame spread over solid materials in microgravity. These studies help improve scientific understanding of spacecraft fire hazards.

📊

Data-Based Insights

Measurements of oxygen, carbon dioxide and carbon monoxide can provide information about experimental conditions and combustion products. Reliable safety conclusions require verified datasets, calibration information and experiment-specific context.

Key Safety Principles

Scientific research supports several general principles for understanding spacecraft fire safety.

1. Study material flammability under relevant atmospheric and airflow conditions.

2. Consider the influence of ventilation on flame spread and smoke transport.

3. Use verified experimental data and validated models when evaluating fire hazards.

4. Treat educational simulations as visual explanations, not certified safety predictions.
